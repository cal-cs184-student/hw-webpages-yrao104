Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation + Primitive Intersection

To generate rays from the camera perspective, we first transformed the given image coordinates to the camera space using the formulas mapping the image coordinates to the sensor range. Specifically, we used the formulas for the bottom left corner and top right corner of the virtual camera sensor to help with this. Then, we transformed the ray to the world space by multiplying it by c2w, the camera-to-world rotation matrix. Lastly, we set the min_t and max_t values to nClip and fClip. Next, to generate pixel samples, we looped through ns_aa samples of camera rays and generated samples from the gridSampler. We then normalized the sample coordinates by the width and height of the image, estimated the global radiance, and finally averaged the cumulative value. Primitive intersection essentially samples the ray along the scene to find the closest intersection point from min_t to max_t.

Triangle Intersection Algorithm

We utilized the Moller Trumbore algorithm for triangle intersection. Using the formula, we calculated the scalar values for t, b_1, and b_2. To check whether the ray was in the triangle, we ensured that the denominator dot product between s_1 and e_1 was not equal to 0, b1 and b2 were >= 0, t was in the ray bounds, and b1 + b2 was <= 1. To calculate the surface normal of the intersection, we used the formula (1 - b_1 - b_2)*n_1 + b_1*n_2 + b_2*n_3.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction Algorithm

We constructed the BVH by first initializing a new BVHNode with the computed bounding box of primitives from start to end. Next, if there are no more than max_leaf_size primitives in the list, we create a leaf node with start and end iterators. Else, we split along the axis with the most spread out primitives by looking at the extent of the centroids. To pick the splitting point, we calculated the midpoint of the centroid bounds. Using this splitting point, we separated the primitives across the left and right child nodes and recursively called the function on these left and right children to repeat this process.

Rendering Times With and Without BVH Acceleration

Rendering scenes with moderately complex geometries took much longer without BVH acceleration. For instance, rendering /dae/sky/CBdragon.dae took 94.9529 seconds without BVH acceleration versus 0.0188 seconds with BVH acceleration. In addition, rendering /dae/sky/CBbunny.dae took 17.0464 seconds without BVH acceleration versus 0.0217 seconds with BVH acceleration. Also, rendering /dae/sky/CBgems.dae took 0.1013 seconds without BVH acceleration versus 0.0219 seconds with BVH acceleration. This shows a speedup ranging from 5x faster for simpler scenes to over 5000x for more complex scenes with the use of BVH acceleration. This depicts how powerful BVH acceleration can be especially on a large scale.
